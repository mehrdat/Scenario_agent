# Synopsis — The Last Cheetah You've Never Heard Of

**Runtime:** 9:30 · **Aspect:** 16:9 · **Voice:** BBC omniscient → Mehrdad personal (outro only)

---

## ACT ONE — The Misdirection and the Reveal (0:00 – 1:50)

The film opens on the image every viewer expects: a cheetah at full sprint across golden African savanna, dust kicked from her paws, a gazelle bolting in the frame ahead. The narrator's voice — calm, measured, in the cadence the world has learned from David Attenborough — begins to describe the fastest land animal on Earth. For eight seconds, this is a film we have seen before.

Then the image cuts. The savanna becomes a stone plateau under a colder sun. The grass becomes scrub. The horizon is no longer Kenyan acacia but the dry mountain ridges of central Iran. The narrator pauses — and asks the audience to imagine a cheetah that does not live where they think.

This is the **Asiatic cheetah** — *Acinonyx jubatus venaticus*, the Yuz (یوز) — and there are fewer than thirty of them left in the world. All of them are here. All of them are Iranian.

A short, controlled passage of comparative biology follows: the Yuz is leaner than her African cousin, longer-legged, with a thicker winter coat and a smaller skull. She is built not for grass but for **stone**. The narrator names the prey: jebeer gazelle, wild sheep, hare. Names the elevation: 1,000 to 3,000 metres. Names the climate: snow in winter, 45°C in summer. The film has now told the audience what it is about. The misdirection is closed. The wound opens.

## ACT TWO — The World, the Wound, the Names (1:50 – 8:30)

**The world (1:50 – 3:00).** The narrator establishes the geography that has both protected and isolated this cat: the protected areas of **Touran, Miandasht, Naybandan, Kavir**. Camera-trap footage shows a single male moving along a dry riverbed at three in the morning. The cheetah is presented not as a species but as a place — a creature inseparable from a specific Iranian topography that most of the world has never seen on screen.

**The wound (3:00 – 5:00).** The film turns historical. A century ago the Asiatic cheetah ranged from the Arabian Peninsula to India; the last Indian cheetahs were shot in 1948. In Iran, the Shah's hunting culture, then the chaos of the 1979 revolution, then the eight-year war with Iraq, then the explosive growth of road infrastructure across central Iran — each blow named and dated. The single deadliest cause of Yuz death in the twenty-first century is shown plainly: vehicle collision. The highway between Tehran and Mashhad cuts directly through the species' last viable habitat. Archival photographs of dead cheetahs at roadsides are held just long enough to register, not long enough to exploit.

**The names (5:00 – 7:00).** This is the emotional spine of the film. Six names, each given the dignity of a portrait card and a story:

**Kooshki**, the male captured young and raised in semi-captivity to seed a breeding programme. **Delbar**, the female who would not breed for years and then, against every expectation, did. **Iran**, the female named for her country. **Koushki** (a second), and **Telma**, the matriarch whose tracking-collar data drew the first true map of how a Yuz uses her land.

And then **Pirouz** (پیروز — *Victorious*) — the cub born on **1 May 2022**, the first Asiatic cheetah born in captivity, broadcast to a nation that had not had good news about its wildlife in a generation. For three hundred days, Pirouz was a small, public miracle. Children drew him. State television covered his weight gain. His mother's milk supply, his vaccination schedule, his first solid food — all of it was followed by a country watching its own hope grow whiskers.

**The All-Is-Lost beat lands at approximately 6:30.** Pirouz dies of kidney failure at ten months. The film does not soften this. A still photograph of the empty incubator. A held silence. The narrator: *He was named Victorious.*

**The dark night (7:00 – 8:30).** Conservation today is harder than it has ever been. International sanctions starve the programme of veterinary equipment. Two senior Iranian wildlife biologists are imprisoned on espionage charges related to their camera-trap work — Kavous Seyed-Emami dies in custody in 2018. The 2026 regional war pulls budgets, attention, and personnel away from the protected areas. The narrator does not editorialise; she states. The audience is allowed to feel.

## ACT THREE — The Final Image (8:30 – 9:30)

The narrator falls silent. The film cuts to **Mehrdad**, the filmmaker, sitting in his own room, in his own daylight, looking directly into the lens. He speaks in his own voice — quieter, unscripted in cadence, personal. He says: *I made this because I am Iranian, and because almost no one outside Iran knows we have a cheetah, and because if you, watching this, never know her name, she will go quietly.*

He names what is known: as of this year, **a single wild female has been observed with five cubs** in Touran National Park. The film closes on grainy, real camera-trap footage of those cubs — moving in single file along a ridge at dusk, their mother turning once to check on them — and on a final title card with the six names, and Pirouz's dates, and a seventh blank line where the next name will go.

Cut to black.
