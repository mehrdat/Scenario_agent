### 1. Scenario craft (سناریونویسی)

#### Development pipeline
`concept → premise → logline → synopsis → treatment → beat sheet → sequence outline → scene list → full script → polish`

Never skip steps — each level catches a different class of problem. Structural failures discovered in the beat sheet cost one day; the same failure discovered in the full script costs three weeks.

#### Structural models — know all, deploy the right one

**Three-act structure (Field / McKee) — the foundation**
The dominant model for commercial cinema. Page references for a 110-page feature:
- **Act I** (pp. 1–25, ~25%): establish protagonist's world and flaw → inciting incident (pp. 10–15) → protagonist commits to the story's central question → **Plot Point 1 / Act break** (pp. 25–27): the world turns upside down, no going back.
- **Act II** (pp. 27–85, ~50%): progressive complications escalate. **Pinch 1** (p. 37): reminder of antagonistic force. **Midpoint** (p. 55): false victory *or* false defeat that reverses the momentum; stakes double. **Pinch 2** (p. 75): antagonist's maximum pressure. **All-Is-Lost** (p. 75–80): the protagonist's worst moment. **Dark Night of the Soul**: internal reckoning. **Plot Point 2 / Act break** (pp. 80–85): revelation or decision that unlocks Act III.
- **Act III** (pp. 85–110, ~25%): protagonist armed with new understanding confronts antagonist → **climax**: the ultimate expression of the theme → **resolution**: new equilibrium.
- Short film (10 pp): incident p. 2, PP1 p. 3, midpoint p. 5, dark moment p. 7, climax p. 9.

**Blake Snyder's Save the Cat — 15 beats (commercial precision)**
| Beat | Page | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Opening image | 1 | Snapshot of protagonist's flawed world; will flip at final image |
| Theme stated | 5 | The film's lesson, said obliquely by a supporting character |
| Setup | 1–10 | Six things that need fixing in the protagonist's life |
| Catalyst | 12 | The inciting incident — life will never be the same |
| Debate | 12–25 | Protagonist resists the call; last chance to turn back |
| Break into Two | 25 | Protagonist chooses to act; enters a new world |
| B Story | 30 | The love story, the friendship, the mentor — teaches the theme |
| Fun & Games | 30–55 | "Promise of the premise" — why audiences bought tickets |
| Midpoint | 55 | False victory → stakes raised; *or* false defeat → protagonist must try harder |
| Bad Guys Close In | 55–75 | External antagonism + internal dissolution |
| All Is Lost | 75 | The whiff of death — everything the protagonist had is gone |
| Dark Night of the Soul | 75–85 | Deepest despair; the breakthrough hasn't happened yet |
| Break into Three | 85 | The "aha" — usually catalyzed by the B Story |
| Finale | 85–110 | Storm the castle; execute the new world order in five steps: gather team → execute plan → plan fails → dig deep → climax |
| Final image | 110 | Proof of transformation; mirrors the opening image |

**Dan Harmon's Story Circle (8 steps — TV, short, serial)**
1. A character is in a zone of comfort (You).
2. They want something (Need).
3. They enter an unfamiliar situation (Go).
4. They adapt to it (Search).
5. They get what they wanted (Find).
6. They pay a heavy price for it (Take).
7. They return to their familiar situation (Return).
8. Having changed (Changed).
Each episode of a series can run its own complete circle. A season arc runs a larger circle across all episodes.

**The Hero's Journey (Campbell / Vogler — 12 stages)**
Ordinary World → Call to Adventure → Refusal of the Call → Meeting the Mentor → Crossing the First Threshold → Tests / Allies / Enemies → Approach to the Inmost Cave → **The Ordeal** (death-and-rebirth; the film's emotional center) → **The Reward** → The Road Back → **The Resurrection** (a second, more public ordeal) → Return with the Elixir.
The Ordeal ≈ midpoint. The Resurrection ≈ climax. The Elixir = what the protagonist brings back for the community, proving the journey mattered.

**Kishōtenketsu (起承転結) — 4-act, no conflict required**
- **Ki** (起 / آغاز): establish characters, setting, situation.
- **Shō** (承 / گسترش): develop naturally from Ki, deepen the world.
- **Ten** (転 / چرخش): unexpected twist that recontextualizes everything — this is not a conflict, it is a *revelation of relationship*.
- **Ketsu** (結 / پیوند): harmonize the contrast; a resolution that honors the twist.
Best for: slice-of-life, nature documentary, essay film, poetic shorts, Iranian art-cinema in the Kiarostami tradition. Do **not** force conflict into a story that does not need it.

**The Sequence Approach (Frank Daniel — sag diagnosis)**
Feature = 8 sequences × 10–15 min, each with its own dramatic question and mini-arc:
- Seq. 1: status quo + hook. Seq. 2: protagonist's first response to inciting incident.
- Seq. 3–4: adapting to the new situation (Act II-A).
- Seq. 5: midpoint shift changes the goal or the stakes.
- Seq. 6: protagonist's reaction, raising the game.
- Seq. 7: crisis + all-is-lost.
- Seq. 8: climax + resolution.
Use this model to find the "Act II sag": if sequences 3–4 don't have their own tight dramatic questions, they will drag. Prescribe: give each sequence a local problem to solve.

**Five-act (Shakespeare / prestige TV)**
- I: Exposition. II: Rising action to complication. III: Climax (of the rising action). IV: Falling action / consequence. V: Catastrophe or resolution.
- TV adaptation: pilot = Act I. Mid-season finale = Act III climax. Season finale = Act V. Each episode = its own mini-five-act.

#### Core craft concepts

**Controlling idea (McKee)**
One sentence: "The story argues that [VALUE] triumphs / is destroyed because [CAUSE]."
Examples: "Justice triumphs because the hero sacrifices personal gain." / "Love is destroyed because of fear of vulnerability." Every scene, every line of dialogue must serve this idea. If you cannot state it, the story doesn't know what it's about.

**The logline formula**
`[Protagonist + defining flaw or desire] must [central action / goal] against [antagonistic force / obstacle], or else [worst-case consequence / stakes].`
Test: does it name who, what they want, what stands in the way, and what's at risk if they fail? Missing any component = structural gap.

**The inciting incident (McKee's four qualities)**
1. Radically upsets the balance of the protagonist's life (positive or negative).
2. Creates the *central dramatic question* the audience keeps asking until the climax.
3. Plants the root of the climax — the inciting incident and climax must mirror each other.
4. Determines the genre: the *type* of disruption defines what *kind* of story this is.
The inciting incident must happen *on screen*. If it happened before the film began, the story starts in the wrong place.

**The gap**
Between the protagonist's expectation of an action's result and the actual result lies *the gap*. A gap forces a new choice under pressure. The larger the gap, the stronger the scene. Predictable results (no gap) = no drama. The audience lives in the gap, leaning forward.

**Setup and payoff (Chekhov's gun principle)**
Every gun shown in Act I *must* fire in Act III. Every payoff needs a setup planted 3–7 scenes earlier. Build a *setup/payoff grid* for every script: column A = the plant, column B = the payoff scene. Unresolved plants = broken promises. Unearned payoffs = deus ex machina.

**Scene construction — the micro-drama**
- **Late in, early out**: enter at the last possible moment before the turning point; leave before the dust settles. Never show: travel, arrivals, greetings, departures, or explanatory preamble unless each carries dramatic weight.
- **Value charge shift**: every scene must begin at one value state (positive / negative / life / death / love / hate) and end at a measurably *different* charge. A scene that ends where it began is a non-scene — cut it or merge it.
- **Beat structure**: scene = sequence of beats. Each beat = unit of behaviour. Within a beat: (1) protagonist's tactic, (2) antagonist's counter, (3) shift in power. A new beat begins when one party changes tactic.
- **Objective / obstacle / tactic**: every character in every scene wants something specific, something stands in the way, and they deploy a specific tactic. If you cannot name the tactic, the scene is vague.

**Progressive complications**
Each obstacle must cost more than the last. The protagonist's first strategy must fail, forcing them to risk something bigger. If the protagonist can solve problems 1, 2, and 3 with the same tool, the escalation is fake. True escalation = higher stakes + greater personal cost + shrinking options.

**Dramatic irony, suspense, mystery (information architecture)**
- **Dramatic irony**: audience knows what the character does not → emotional tension (dread, anticipation, pity).
- **Suspense**: audience and character both don't know → anxiety.
- **Mystery / enigma**: audience knows *less* than the character → curiosity.
- **Surprise**: audience knows nothing, event reveals suddenly → jolt (use sparingly; it fades fast).
Design the *information flow* scene by scene. Which characters know what, and when does the audience learn it?

**Subtext**
What people *don't* say is the story. Subtext = the gap between what a character says and what they mean, what they want, what they fear. On-the-nose dialogue (characters say exactly what they feel) destroys subtext. Diagnosis: if a line could be cut and replaced by a stage direction reading "she tells him she loves him" — it's on the nose. Cure: the character *does* something instead.

**The B story**
The B story (subplot) is not decoration. Its job: teach the protagonist — and the audience — the theme the protagonist is too stubborn to accept in the A story. The B story and A story must converge at the Break into Three, where the B story insight enables the protagonist to solve the A story problem.

### 2. Character design (شخصیت‌پردازی)

#### The four-layer architecture (K.M. Weiland / McKee)

**Layer 1 — The external want (conscious objective)**
What the character *thinks* they want. Specific, concrete, achievable in principle. The engine of the plot. The audience can track it. Example: "Win the court case." "Get the girl back." "Find my father."

**Layer 2 — The internal need (unconscious wound)**
What the character *actually needs* to become whole — and which directly conflicts with the want. The engine of the *theme*. The protagonist usually only understands the need at the climax. Example: "Learn to trust." "Accept mortality." "Stop performing and be honest."

The dramatic engine is the **want / need collision**. The story forces the protagonist to choose, at the climax, between getting the want or fulfilling the need. In a positive-arc story, they choose the need. In a tragedy, they choose the want (and are destroyed or diminished).

**Layer 3 — The ghost / wound**
The *specific past event* that created the misbelief. Must be dated, sensory, particular. Not "a difficult childhood" — "the afternoon of Nowruz 1368 when her father locked her out of the house in the rain because she had come second in class." This level of specificity makes the wound real. The ghost is what the story is *actually* about, even if it is never spoken.

**Layer 4 — The lie the character believes**
One sentence, first person. The misbelief installed by the ghost that the story must test and ultimately break or confirm. "I am only valuable when I succeed." "Closeness always leads to abandonment." "My country will never change." The theme of the story is the argument *against* (or for) this lie.

#### Character arc types

**Positive change arc**: protagonist recognizes the lie, abandons it, embraces the truth, transformed.
**Flat / steadfast arc**: protagonist already holds the truth; the story is about the *world* being changed by contact with the protagonist (most action heroes; many mentors).
**Negative arc / corruption**: protagonist starts close to the truth, is broken down, ends embracing the lie. Tragic. Used in cautionary tales (*Breaking Bad*, classic tragedy).
**Disillusionment arc**: protagonist believes something positive, discovers it is false, ends in a more sober but truthful place. Not the same as tragedy — this is often the most honest arc.
**Fall-then-rise (redemption arc)**: protagonist deep in the lie at opening, descends further, then breaks through.

#### Psychological scaffolding (tools, not cages)

**Enneagram (9 types + wings)**
Each type has a core fear, core desire, and a characteristic vice:
- **1 — The Reformer**: fear of corruption; desire to be good; vice: resentment. *Voice*: precise, judgmental, reforming.
- **2 — The Helper**: fear of being unloved; desire to be needed; vice: manipulation disguised as service. *Voice*: warm, slightly martyred.
- **3 — The Achiever**: fear of worthlessness; desire to be valuable; vice: deceit (even self-deceit). *Voice*: efficient, promotional, image-aware.
- **4 — The Individualist**: fear of having no identity; desire to be unique; vice: envy. *Voice*: intense, metaphoric, emotionally florid.
- **5 — The Investigator**: fear of helplessness; desire to be competent; vice: avarice (hoarding knowledge/energy). *Voice*: spare, observational, withheld.
- **6 — The Loyalist**: fear of abandonment; desire to have guidance; vice: anxiety. *Voice*: questioning, self-doubting, looking for authority.
- **7 — The Enthusiast**: fear of pain / deprivation; desire to be satisfied; vice: gluttony. *Voice*: rapid, associative, topic-jumping, reframing dark as light.
- **8 — The Challenger**: fear of being controlled; desire to be autonomous; vice: lust for power. *Voice*: blunt, commanding, confrontational.
- **9 — The Peacemaker**: fear of conflict; desire for harmony; vice: sloth (disengagement from self). *Voice*: slow, agreeable, vague about own opinions.
Use the Enneagram to generate *automatic conflict*: put a 1 and a 7 in a room; they will conflict without the writer forcing it.

**Jungian archetypes (Vogler)**
- **Hero**: the protagonist — not a superhero, a person who grows.
- **Mentor / Wise Old Man or Woman**: gives the hero gifts, prepares them for the ordeal.
- **Threshold Guardian**: tests the hero's commitment before each new world.
- **Herald**: signals change is coming; catalyzes the call to adventure.
- **Shapeshifter**: ally who seems unreliable; usually a romantic interest or double agent; represents ambiguity.
- **Shadow**: the antagonist; often the hero's dark mirror (what the hero could become).
- **Trickster**: provides comic relief and *perspective*; often says the theme out loud while joking.
- **Ally**: reflects the hero's change by changing alongside them.

**Maslow's hierarchy for stakes**
Characters fight for what they're missing. Identify where the protagonist sits on the hierarchy:
1. Physiological (survival) → war films, disaster, survival horror.
2. Safety (security, order) → thriller, crime, domestic drama.
3. Love/belonging → romance, family drama, ensemble.
4. Esteem (respect, achievement) → sports, biopic, workplace drama.
5. Self-actualisation → art film, coming-of-age, existential drama.
The higher the tier, the more *internal* the conflict. The lower the tier, the more *external*. The most powerful films work both tiers simultaneously.

#### Voice differentiation
No two characters in the same script should sound alike. Differentiate on *five axes*:
1. **Vocabulary level**: educated / street / technical / archaic / regional.
2. **Sentence length**: clipped monosyllables vs. long flowing subordinate clauses.
3. **Idiolect markers**: 3 recurring phrases, fillers, or tics unique to this character. They never share a tic with another character.
4. **Relationship to truth**: does this character say what they mean (direct), deflect (avoid), lie (perform), or overstate (dramatize)?
5. **Taboo zone**: 2–3 topics or words this character systematically avoids. What they *won't* say reveals more than what they say.

#### Physical life and silhouette
- **Silhouette test**: can you identify the character from a black cutout? Distinct posture, clothing shape, and signature prop. A character without a silhouette doesn't exist visually.
- **Gait**: how a person walks is how they relate to the world. Hesitant = internal locus. Confident but jerky = controlled aggression. Fluid = ease with body and environment.
- **Signature prop**: the one object that tells the character's story (the rosary beads, the cigarette not lit, the notebook always carried but never opened).
- **Costume colour palette**: 3 hex codes per character. Colour arcs across the film: protagonist moves from desaturated grey to warm amber as they grow; antagonist holds static red-black. Never dress two important characters in the same colour in the same scene.

#### Relationship matrix
Map every pair of characters with: power differential (who has leverage), history (shared past event), desire direction (what each wants from the other), and the hidden secret between them. This matrix generates automatic subtext in every shared scene.

### 3. Dialogue (گفتگو)

#### The five functions of dialogue
A line of dialogue is only worth keeping if it performs at least one of these functions — ideally two or three simultaneously:
1. **Reveal character**: the *way* a character says something tells us who they are.
2. **Advance the plot**: new information or decision that changes the story's direction.
3. **Establish or deepen relationship**: shifts the power, intimacy, or conflict between two characters.
4. **Carry subtext**: says one thing, means another — the gap between surface and depth.
5. **Set or sustain tone**: positions the audience's emotional register (comic, dread, intimacy, irony).

If a line only establishes setting or backstory, cut it and find a visual alternative.

#### Subtext techniques
- **Deflection**: character is asked about X and answers about Y. The evasion *is* the information.
- **Tangent**: character starts toward the truth, swerves to a memory, an object, a grievance. The tangent reveals the wound.
- **Non-sequitur**: character's response seems disconnected — but the audience reads the emotional logic beneath it.
- **Silence and pause**: Harold Pinter's "pause" and "silence" have precise meanings. A pause is a disruption in dialogue; a silence is a breakdown. Both are readable. Train yourself to write what the *body* does in those silences.
- **Understatement**: the Iranian master of this is Kiarostami. A character says "It's a nice day" standing at a grave. The gap between statement and context *is* the emotion.
- **Over-explanation**: a character who explains too much is hiding something. The over-explanation points straight at the suppressed truth.

#### Mamet's rules (Practical Cinematics)
- Every scene: someone wants something, someone else doesn't want them to have it.
- Every line is a tactic to get that thing.
- Cut every line that doesn't serve the objective.
- Characters don't listen to each other — they wait to deploy their next tactic. This is realistic and dramatically efficient.
- Use *actions* (verbs) to describe what a character is doing beneath the words: "she *entices*", "he *deflects*", "she *threatens*", "he *bribes*".

#### Aaron Sorkin's intention-obstacle structure
Every scene has: an intention (what the protagonist wants *in this scene*) and an obstacle (what prevents them from getting it immediately). The scene is the negotiation between intention and obstacle. If there's no obstacle, there's no scene. Walk-and-talks work because the physical environment creates a third character — the space itself becomes an obstacle or a collaborator.

#### Elmore Leonard's 10 rules for dialogue
1. Never open a book with weather. (In dialogue: never open a scene with small talk.)
2. Avoid prologues. (In dialogue: cut the warm-up.)
3. Never use a verb other than "said" to carry dialogue. (In action lines: trust the words.)
4. Never use an adverb to modify the verb "said". ("She said angrily" → cut. Show the anger.)
5. Keep exclamation marks rare.
6. Never use "suddenly".
7. Use regional dialect sparingly — a character's voice, not a phonetic transcription.
8. Avoid detailed descriptions of characters (the director will ignore them; let personality emerge through speech).
9. Don't go into great detail about places and things.
10. Leave out the parts readers skip. (Cut everything the audience doesn't *need*.)

#### Idiomatic Farsi dialogue — specific skills
- **Ta'arof (تعارف)**: the Persian system of ritualized politeness — an offer that both parties understand is not a sincere offer. Writing ta'arof dialogue requires showing when a character accepts what should be refused, and what that violation signals. Ta'arof is a system of *delayed honesty* and functions as a mine-field of subtext.
- **Generational register shift**: a 70-year-old grandmother in Tehran speaks literary Farsi with Arabic loanwords; her 20-year-old grandchild speaks Finglish (Persian + English hybrid). Putting them in dialogue without register shift is a craft failure.
- **Indirect address of taboo**: Iranian social culture avoids direct address of death, illness, sexuality, political opinion, and money. Characters circle these topics through metaphor, religious phrase, proverb, or sudden topic change. This indirection is not weakness — it is the subtext engine of Iranian drama.
- **Regional dialect cues**: drop 3–5 dialect markers per regional character. Not a phonetic transcription — a flavour. Esfahanis use specific intonation patterns and vocabulary; Tabrizis code-switch to Azeri; Shirazi speech has distinct rhythm.
- **Persian proverb as subtext**: a character who quotes a proverb at a charged moment is using an inherited cultural script to say something they cannot say directly. This is a powerful subtext device. Use it precisely, not decoratively.

#### Documentary interview question design
The best documentary interviews are built on questions that feel simple but are structurally demanding:
- **Open "take me back" questions**: "Tell me about the day you first realized …" (activates episodic memory, produces vivid language).
- **Sensory anchoring**: "What did it smell like?" "What were you wearing?" Sensory detail unlocks authentic recall.
- **The "what did you think at the time / what do you think now?" split**: reveals arc and growth without asking about arc and growth.
- **The productive silence**: ask a question, then wait 10 full seconds after the subject finishes. Most people fill silence — and the filling is often the most honest answer.
- **The cycling-back question**: "Earlier you said X. But you also said Y. How do those two things live in the same person?" Forces integration and often produces the most revealing answer.
- **Never ask yes/no questions**: rephrase all yes/no questions to "how" or "tell me about."
- **The "impossible" question**: ask something you know they can't answer simply. "Why did you stay?" The struggle to answer *is* the content.

### 4. Visual plan & shot design (طرح بصری و دکوپاژ)

#### Shot size taxonomy (with dramatic use)
| Label | Frame includes | Dramatic function |
|---|---|---|
| **ELS** — Extreme Long Shot | character tiny in environment | isolation, epic scale, geography as character |
| **LS** — Long Shot | full body with headroom | introduction, physical action, relationship to space |
| **MLS** — Medium Long Shot | waist to above head | social interaction, status display |
| **MS** — Medium Shot | waist up | neutral coverage, conversation |
| **MCU** — Medium Close-Up | chest up | intimacy, personal stakes |
| **CU** — Close-Up | face only | emotional revelation, internal state, the turning point |
| **ECU** — Extreme Close-Up | eyes, mouth, hands, objects | micro-gesture, detail that changes meaning of a scene |

**Coverage strategy**: master shot (LS/MLS) establishes geography → singles (MS/MCU) handle dialogue → CUs mark emotional turning points → inserts (ECU) plant or pay off story elements. Never use a CU until you have earned it with narrative weight.

#### Lens physics and their emotional effects
| Focal length | Depth of field | Perspective | Emotional register |
|---|---|---|---|
| 14–18mm | Deep (everything sharp) | Distorted, expansive, vertigo | Instability, scope, alienation |
| 24–28mm | Deep | Natural wide — human eye's peripheral field | Intimacy in wide spaces; surveillance quality |
| 35mm | Medium | The "street" lens — natural, invisible | Observational, journalistic, realistic |
| 50mm | Medium | Closest to human foveal vision | Neutral, classical, authoritative |
| 85mm | Shallow | Slight compression, face pops from background | Portrait, desire, admiration — the "beautiful" lens |
| 135mm | Very shallow | Strong compression, backgrounds flatten | Isolation in a crowd, pressure, surveillance |
| 200mm+ | Razor-thin | Extreme compression, subjects appear trapped | Paranoia, distance, entrapment |

**Depth of field as storytelling**: sharp background = character integrated into world. Blurred background = character isolated. Rack focus from foreground to background = audience re-directed to a new dramatic element; rack from sharp background to sharp foreground = the new element has arrived in the world.

#### Camera movement and meaning
Every camera move must be *motivated* — by character psychology, by story information, or by established visual grammar of the film. Unmotivated camera moves call attention to the camera (acceptable only in reflexive or self-aware films).
- **Static**: power, authority, observation, patience; used for the most important moments.
- **Pan**: follows action; or reveals something alongside or beyond the subject.
- **Tilt up**: ascension, ambition, reveal of scale; **tilt down**: gravity, shame, diminishment.
- **Dolly in**: intimacy increasing, revelation, the world closing in.
- **Dolly out**: withdrawal, loss, the world expanding away from the subject.
- **Dolly zoom (Hitchcock / Vertigo)**: dolly out + zoom in simultaneously = spatial distortion = psychological vertigo, dread, the uncanny.
- **Tracking / trucking**: follows movement; creates rhythm and forward momentum.
- **Crane / jib up**: god's-eye view, liberation, departure; **crane down**: arrival, oppression, revelation from above.
- **Handheld**: urgency, chaos, intimacy, documentarian presence; overuse destroys effect.
- **Gimbal**: controlled yet fluid — modern naturalism.
- **Drone**: aerial geography, surveillance, scale; the first drone shot must justify why we see from the sky.
- **Arc / orbit**: circles a subject → obsession, inspection, threat, revelation of a new dimension.

#### The 180° rule and screen direction
The camera must stay on one side of the 180° line in any two-character scene. Cross the line and the characters appear to swap sides — spatial coherence breaks. Deliberate line-crossing is a valid choice to signal disorientation, power shift, or psychological break — but it must be earned, not accidental. Screen direction (character moving left-to-right = goal-oriented forward motion; right-to-left = regression, return, retreat) applies across entire films, not just scenes.

#### Lighting plan vocabulary
**Three-point setup**:
- **Key light**: primary source; determines the look. Hard (point source = dramatic, contrasty, noir) vs. soft (diffused = naturalistic, accessible).
- **Fill light**: reduces shadows. The key-to-fill ratio determines mood: 4:1 = high drama; 2:1 = naturalistic; 1:1 = flat commercial.
- **Back light / hair light / rim light**: separates subject from background; gives depth and dimension.

**Motivated sources**: every light should appear to come from a logical source (window, lamp, fire, screen, streetlight). Unmotivated light draws the eye to the illusion. Practicals (real bulbs visible in frame) anchor the motivated source.

**Colour temperature as emotion**:
- Warm (2700–3500K, tungsten, golden hour): intimacy, memory, safety, nostalgia.
- Neutral (5000–5500K, daylight): documentary truth, objectivity, clarity.
- Cool (6500–8000K, shade, blue sky): alienation, modernity, grief, surveillance.
- Mixed (two sources of different temperatures in the same frame): tension, instability, hybrid identity.

**Lighting per genre**: high-key (bright, minimal shadow) = comedy, musical, romance. Low-key (dominant shadow) = noir, thriller, horror, tragedy. Chiaroscuro (extreme contrast) = moral ambiguity, Renaissance painting quality.

**Magic hour / golden hour**: the 20–40 minutes after sunrise and before sunset. Light is warm, directional, soft. Creates beauty almost automatically — which means it is *too easy*; use it to signal a story moment that deserves beauty.

**Blue hour**: the 15–20 minutes after sunset before dark. Electric blue, low contrast, liminal quality. Signals: transition, the between-worlds moment, endings, departure.

#### Colour script (Pixar method, applied to live action)
Design the palette per act before shooting:
- **Act I** colour: the world before change. Establish the emotional temperature.
- **Act II-A** colour: the "promise of the premise" world. Often brighter or more saturated than Act I.
- **Midpoint colour shift**: a noticeably different palette signals the story has changed register.
- **Act II-B / All-Is-Lost colour**: desaturated, cooler, or darker.
- **Act III / Climax colour**: synthesis — often the protagonist's colour and the antagonist's colour collide or merge.
- **Final image colour**: bookends the opening. If the protagonist has changed, the palette shifts in their favour.

#### Composition principles
- **Rule of thirds**: place subjects and horizons at the intersection of the grid, not dead centre (unless centred framing is used for symmetry / Wes Anderson / power).
- **Leading lines**: roads, fences, corridors, gutters — any line that draws the eye toward or away from the subject.
- **Frame within frame**: windows, doors, arches, mirrors — creates depth and frames the subject as observed, trapped, or precious.
- **Symmetry and asymmetry**: symmetry = order, control, oppression, authority. Asymmetry = chaos, dynamism, tension.
- **Headroom and lead room**: headroom = space above the head. Lead room = space in front of the direction of gaze. Violate both rules intentionally to create claustrophobia or wrongness.
- **Depth layers**: foreground / midground / background. Objects in all three layers = richness and immersion. Single plane = flatness (use deliberately for alienation or graphic style).
- **Negative space**: empty space around a character signals loneliness, freedom, or potential. Fill the frame = pressure, claustrophobia.

#### Editing principles (you write the cut in the script)
- **Montage — Eisenstein's 5 types**: Metric (cuts on rhythm), Rhythmic (image length matches content), Tonal (cuts on mood), Overtonal (combination of the above), Intellectual (juxtaposition creates a new idea the images alone don't contain — the Kuleshov effect).
- **The Kuleshov effect**: the shot before and after a neutral face *creates* the emotion the audience reads. The cut is the filmmaker's instrument of control over meaning.
- **Match cut**: two shapes, movements, or sounds rhyme across the cut — creates visual poetry and narrative connection.
- **Jump cut**: breaks continuity deliberately — Godard's tool for disruption, urgency, refusal of illusionism.
- **Cross-cutting (parallel editing)**: two or more scenes intercut — implies simultaneity, creates tension through comparison.
- **The L-cut and J-cut**: audio from the next scene begins before we cut visually (L-cut pulls us forward); audio from the previous scene continues over the new image (J-cut anchors us in the previous emotional state before the new setting lands). Both are invisible and elegant.
- **The reaction shot**: in dialogue, *who we cut to* and *when* tells the audience who is really being affected by what is being said.

#### Sound design (write it in the scene description)
- **Diegetic sound**: exists in the story world (character can hear it). Car engine, conversation in background, music on a radio.
- **Non-diegetic sound**: score and narration only the audience hears.
- **Contrapuntal sound**: sound and image in opposition — upbeat music over violence; silence over beauty. Creates ironic distance and emotional complexity.
- **Silence as punctuation**: a sudden cut to silence (after music or ambient noise) is more powerful than any sound effect. Use at turning points and revelations.
- **Off-screen sound**: what we *hear* but do not *see* is the imagination of the audience, which is always more powerful than what you show them.

### 8. Genre mastery — detailed per-genre mechanics (گونه‌شناسی)

#### Fiction genres — obligatory scenes and emotional promises

**Drama**
*Emotional promise*: the audience will be moved by recognizing something true about human experience.
*Obligatory scenes*: (1) the wound revealed — the protagonist's ghost surfaces. (2) The moral dilemma — an impossible choice between two values the protagonist holds. (3) The moment of grace — a small, unrequested act of kindness that cracks the protagonist's armour. (4) The confrontation — protagonist faces what they have been avoiding. (5) The earned ending — resolution that feels inevitable in retrospect but was not predictable.
*Pitfalls*: passive protagonist, no escalation, miserabilism without meaning, thematic statement delivered in dialogue.

**Thriller / Psychological Thriller**
*Emotional promise*: suspense, the pleasure of superior information, the fear of a world that is not what it seems.
*Obligatory scenes*: (1) The hook — threat established within 5 minutes. (2) The false solution — protagonist thinks they've solved it; they haven't. (3) The revelation — the true shape of the conspiracy / danger. (4) The ticking clock — deadline established that makes every minute matter. (5) The reversal — the person who seemed safe is dangerous; the person who seemed dangerous is an ally. (6) The final confrontation in the worst possible place.
*Pitfalls*: slow revelations, protagonist is reactive throughout (no agency), the twist that requires the audience to feel cheated.

**Horror**
*Emotional promise*: dread, visceral fear, and the catharsis of surviving something that shouldn't be survivable.
*Modes*: Gothic (atmosphere, dread, the past pressing into the present), Slasher (survival, physical threat, punitive morality), Cosmic / Lovecraftian (the terror of insignificance, knowledge that destroys sanity), Folk (community as threat, ritual, the land itself), Psychological (is the monster real? the horror is the mind).
*Obligatory scenes*: (1) Normality established — so the violation is felt. (2) The first sign — ignored or misread. (3) The escalating threat. (4) The false safety. (5) The all-is-lost horror. (6) The cost of survival (the protagonist is not unharmed).
*The monster as metaphor*: every great horror film is about something. Dracula = predatory aristocracy. The Thing = paranoia during the Cold War. Get Out = liberal racism. Transgression = grief, repressed sexuality, colonial guilt. Identify your film's real subject and let the monster embody it.

**Comedy**
*Emotional promise*: laughter, relief, the pleasure of incongruity resolved.
*Mechanics*: (1) The comic premise = two things that should not be in the same room. (2) The rule of three — set up a pattern twice, break it on the third. (3) Specificity = comedy; abstraction = silence. (4) Timing: comedy is rhythm. The pause before the punchline is the punchline. (5) The callback — a joke planted early, paid off late, transformed by context. (6) Physical vs. verbal: physical comedy reads across languages; verbal comedy is culturally specific (extremely important for Farsi comedy — what is funny in Tehran is not always funny in Mashhad, and is often not translatable at all).
*Farsi comedy tradition*: Luti culture, slapstick rooted in the *Ru-howzi* (حوضی) theatrical tradition, the comic mulla figure, Samad Agha, the self-deprecating provincial, the naive modernizer. These archetypes are bankable if used with affection.

**Romantic Comedy**
*Obligatory beats*: meet-cute → misunderstanding that keeps them apart → "I think I like them" → crisis (the lie / the secret / the rival) → all-is-lost (they seem truly separated) → grand gesture → union.
*The genre promise*: these two people belong together; the story is the pleasure of watching obstacles prevent and then enable that inevitability.
*Pitfalls*: the grand gesture that requires the audience to forget that the protagonist behaved terribly; the "manic pixie dream girl" who has no interiority.

**Sci-fi**
*The "novum" (Darko Suvin)*: every sci-fi story is built on one "novum" — a cognitive estrangement device (a new technology, a new biology, a new social order) that reframes what it means to be human. The novum must be consistent, internally logical, and *extrapolated from something real*. Hard sci-fi (The Martian): scientific accuracy prioritized. Soft sci-fi (Her): technology as metaphor for human need. Cyberpunk: corporate power, identity dissolution, digital/flesh boundary. Solarpunk: hopeful futures, ecological repair, community.
*Obligatory question*: what does this "what if" reveal about humanity *right now*?

**Crime / Noir**
*Noir's essential elements*: the femme fatale or homme fatale (temptation that leads to destruction), the corrupt city, the protagonist who is complicit in the corruption, the MacGuffin (the object everyone wants, that is ultimately meaningless), the cynical worldview ("everyone has a price"), the protagonist's moral compromise.
*Structure*: investigation or heist drives the A story; the protagonist's complicity in the corrupt system drives the theme.

**Historical / Biopic**
*The biopic problem*: a life is not a story. Compress — choose one chapter, one question, one turning point that illuminates the whole. The best biopics are not "the life of X"; they are "the year X made the most important choice of their life."
*Research obligation*: all biographical facts must be sourced or marked `[dramatized]`. The audience trusts the film to tell them something true about a real person.

**Coming-of-age**
*Structure*: the rite of passage — the protagonist crosses from one state (child, innocent, certain) to another (adult, experienced, uncertain). The crossing costs something. The mentor departs. The world does not reward the change immediately.
*Iranian coming-of-age*: the Nowruz threshold (a year closes, a new one opens), military service, the university entrance exam (Konkur), first romantic feeling under surveillance culture — these are specific and resonant pressure points.

### 9. Documentary craft — deep skills (مستندسازی)

#### Bill Nichols' six modes — know which one you're making

| Mode | Characteristics | Masters | When to use |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Expository** | Narration addresses audience directly; images illustrate argument | Ken Burns, David Attenborough | Historical, nature, educational; when authority and clarity are the goal |
| **Observational** | Camera observes without intervention; no narration, no interviews | Frederick Wiseman, the Maysles brothers | When you want the audience to form their own judgments; institution as protagonist |
| **Participatory** | Filmmaker on camera, interacting with subjects; the relationship is the content | Nick Broomfield, Michael Moore (participatory + performative) | When your presence changes what happens; subjective truth |
| **Reflexive** | Film is *about* the act of making the film; calls attention to its own construction | Ross McElwee (*Sherman's March*), Agnès Varda | When you want to interrogate documentary's claims to truth |
| **Performative** | Subjective, evocative; emphasizes filmmaker's emotional and political engagement | Michael Moore, Marlon Riggs | Advocacy, personal essay, political argument |
| **Poetic** | Prioritises mood, tone, image rhythm over argument | Godfrey Reggio (*Koyaanisqatsi*), Terrence Malick docs | When experience is the point, not information |

The most successful contemporary documentaries are *hybrids* — typically participatory + expository, or observational + essay.

#### What makes a documentary popular (audience appeal mechanics)
1. **The impossible subject**: a character or situation so specific, strange, or extreme that it seems like fiction. (*Free Solo*, *Three Identical Strangers*, *Blackfish*). 
2. **The withheld revelation**: structural suspense — the audience knows something is coming that the subjects don't. True-crime structure.
3. **The underdog arc**: a person or group against an institution, fighting for something the audience believes in.
4. **The intimate access**: the filmmaker goes somewhere the audience never could — a prison, a palace, a war zone, a private grief.
5. **The "I didn't know this" factor**: facts or revelations that make the audience feel smarter and more aware. (*Seaspiracy*, *Super Size Me*, *The Social Dilemma*).
6. **Character jeopardy**: a real person the audience cares about is in genuine danger — physical, financial, moral.
7. **The twist that reframes everything**: a documentary that reveals, at the three-quarter mark, that what we thought the film was about is *not* what the film is about.

#### Documentary structure
Most successful documentaries use a modified three-act:
- **Act I**: establish the subject + the question the film is investigating. The "why now" — what is at stake today? The best first 5 minutes: a hook that makes the audience say "I need to know how this ends."
- **Act II**: deepen the investigation. Introduce opposing viewpoints. The protagonist (human subject or filmmaker's inquiry) hits obstacles. The midpoint revelation changes the shape of the question.
- **Act III**: the crisis and its consequence. The "surprising truth" (something the audience did not expect). The ending that does not tie everything up, but gives the audience something to take home.

**Asif Kapadia's archival method (Senna, Amy, Maradona)**:
- No on-camera talking heads.
- All narration comes from archival audio: interviews recorded by others, answering machine messages, radio broadcasts, home video audio.
- The audience experiences the subject through the eyes of the people who knew them.
- The method creates an uncanny intimacy — you are always *with* the subject, never watching an expert explain them.
- To use this method: collect extensive archival audio before scripting. The archive tells you what the film is.

**Kieslowski's rule**: "Find the film while shooting." Documentary structure is discovered, not pre-set. Build flexibility into the plan. The surprise access, the unexpected emotional moment, the subject who says something you couldn't have scripted — these are the film.

#### Interview technique (director's chair)
**Pre-interview**: meet the subject casually before filming. Learn their speech patterns, what topics make them light up, what they avoid. This is your research session — the filmed interview is the performance of what you learned here.

**Room setup**: the subject sits slightly angled to camera (not full-face, not full-profile). Interview them off-axis — looking at you, not the lens. Their eye-line close to the lens = intimate, direct. Eye-line far from lens = observational, distant. Settle on a consistent room look so that each subject interview *coheres* across the edit.

**The Errol Morris interrotron**: a half-silvered mirror allows the subject to look at a projected image of the filmmaker's face *on* the camera lens — the subject is looking directly into the lens while maintaining eye contact with the filmmaker. Creates the directness of a confessional. Achievable on a budget with improvised setups.

**The long question, the long wait**: after the subject answers, wait 8–10 full seconds before the next question. Most people fill silence with their most honest thought. This is where the real interview happens.

**B-roll theory** — 4 types:
1. **Illustrative**: directly shows what the interviewee is describing (archive photo of the event being discussed).
2. **Counterpoint**: shows something in tension with what the interviewee is saying — creates irony and ambiguity.
3. **Question**: an image that poses a question the film is investigating (a locked door, an empty chair, a photograph face-down).
4. **Answer**: an image that resolves a question — use these sparingly, at act breaks and climax.

**The B-roll list**: produce a scene-by-scene B-roll list alongside the interview design. For each section of interview, identify: what illustrates it (archive), what contradicts it (counterpoint), what questions it raises (question B-roll), and what resolves it (answer B-roll). This discipline reveals structural gaps in the argument before the edit.

#### Re-enactment (Errol Morris methodology)
Morris pioneered the serious use of re-enactment in *The Thin Blue Line* (1988). Rules:
- Never deceive the audience into thinking re-enactment *is* archive. Stylize it: slow motion, different film stock look, unusual camera angles.
- Re-enact *interpretation*, not fact. Show what the witness *remembers*, not what definitely happened. Multiple witnesses = multiple re-enactments of the same event, each stylistically different.
- Re-enactment is not reconstruction. It is *argument*.

**Joshua Oppenheimer's *The Act of Killing* extension**: ask perpetrators to re-enact their own actions in the genre of *their choice*. The re-enactment becomes a revelation of psychology: how does a person perform, and thus *understand*, their own history? The gap between the genre they choose and the horror of what they're re-enacting *is the documentary*.

### 10. Geography & cultural context — Iranian stories in depth (جغرافیا و بافت فرهنگی)

#### Physical geography as character
Every region of Iran has a distinct light, architecture, soundscape, pace, and relationship to time. Never let your story float in a generic "Iran."

- **Tehran**: chaos-energy, 15-million-person layer-cake of class and generation. Traffic as external pressure. Roof culture (parties, antennas, escape). The split between north (wealth, secular, Westernized surface) and south (traditional, working-class, religious). The *alley* (کوچه) as social unit — the space between the street and the home where community actually happens.
- **Isfahan (اصفهان)**: the old Safavid capital. Turquoise dome light. The *Zayandeh-Rud* riverbed, now often dry (an ecological grief that is also a metaphor). Armenian Christian quarter (Jolfa) — a story about Iranian pluralism that is usually untold. Slower pace than Tehran, stronger sense of civic pride, different class markers.
- **Shiraz (شیراز)**: literary city, city of Hafez and Saadi. Garden culture. Strong ta'arof. Southern light, brighter and harder. The poetry tradition means that quoting Hafez is everyday speech, not performance — the most powerful subtext device in a Shirazi story.
- **Tabriz (تبریز)**: Azeri-speaking majority; Turkish and Persian identity in tension; closer to Turkey and the Caucasus; the Constitutional Revolution (مشروطه) began here. The *Grand Bazaar* as economic ecosystem and social world. Stories here must hold both languages.
- **Ahvaz (اهواز) and Khuzestan**: Arab-Iranian identity, Arabic dialect, oil industry, extreme heat, the wounds of the Iran-Iraq war that are still being excavated. One of the most under-represented regions in Iranian cinema — immense story potential.
- **Kurdistan / Sanandaj**: mountainous, Kurdish identity, seasonal rhythms (agricultural / nomadic), the specific light of the Zagros — golden and cold. The political silence around Kurdish identity creates rich subtext.
- **Yazd**: the oldest continuously inhabited city in the world. Badgir (wind-tower) architecture. Zoroastrian community, fire temples. Desert light, extreme temperatures, adobe architecture. A sense of geological time.
- **Rasht / Gilan**: lush, rainy, rice-paddy landscape. Gilaki language. Caspian Sea culture. Food-centredness. The *Villa* (ویلا) as social space — where Tehranis go to escape. The rain as constant companion — a cinematographic gift.

#### Cultural mechanics for scenario writing

**Ta'arof (تعارف)** — the full mechanics:
Ta'arof is not simply politeness. It is a *social contract* with specific rules:
- An offer must be made three times before it is considered genuine.
- The first response to any offer must be refusal (even if you want it desperately).
- Accepting on the first offer marks you as uncouth, greedy, or foreign.
- Ta'arof operates in: food, hospitality, payment (who pays at a restaurant is settled through elaborate ta'arof), compliments, requests, apologies.
- **Dramatic use**: ta'arof creates scenes where the *literal* meaning of every line is false, and the real meaning is subtext. A character who breaks ta'arof — accepts the first offer, insists on paying, says what they mean directly — is either: deeply intimate with the other person, a foreigner, in a crisis, or committing an act of aggression.
- **Violation as plot**: when ta'arof breaks down in a scene, something significant is happening. Farhadi builds entire films on ta'arof violations.

**Generations and the 1357 / 1979 fault line**:
- **Pre-revolutionary generation** (born before 1357): lived through the Shah, the revolution, the war. Carry both a monarchist and a revolutionary possible self. Their silence about the past is not evasion — it is survival.
- **The war generation** (born 1340s–1360s / 1961–1981): shaped by the Iran-Iraq war. Many have a brother, cousin, or father who died in it. The war is rarely spoken of directly — its presence is the silence.
- **The post-revolution generation** (born 1360s–1380s / 1981–2001): grew up in the Islamic Republic, have no lived memory of anything else, but are shaped by satellite TV, the internet, and constant comparison between their world and a globalized outside. The generation of *Jinn* (جن) — haunted by the life that might have been.
- **The digital generation** (born after 1380 / 2001): Telegram, Instagram, VPN. Code-switch effortlessly between the public performance of religiosity and the private life of secular urbanity. The most code-switching generation in Iranian history.

**Hijab dynamics in dramatic writing**:
Hijab in Iranian cinema is not a simple binary. It is a spectrum of meaning: the *chador* (full coverage) vs. the *roosari* (headscarf loosely worn) vs. the *bad-hijab* (improperly worn, a statement) vs. the absent hijab (in the private home, which film can show; in public space, which film cannot). In international co-productions, the constraints differ. Always know your exhibition context.

**Censorship and the art of the ellipsis**:
Iranian cinema under censorship developed the ellipsis as a high art form. What cannot be shown *is shown* through: off-screen sound, reaction shots, cuts to objects, poetic metaphor, the camera looking away. This is not a limitation — it is a craft tradition. The Kiarostami-Farhadi school proved that restriction can generate more powerful emotional truth than freedom. Write *around* the constraint and discover what the constraint forces you to find.

**Religious calendar as dramatic structure**:
- **Nowruz** (نوروز, New Year, spring equinox): the great Iranian threshold — a time of family reunions, unresolved tensions, forced festivity. Rich dramatic space for confrontation, forgiveness, and unfinished business.
- **Muharram** (*محرم*, particularly Ashura): public mourning, Hosseini processions, the collective performance of grief. Dramatically potent backdrop for personal grief — the public ritual mirrors or contrasts the private wound.
- **Ramadan**: fasting, altered daily rhythms, social obligations (iftar), the gap between public observance and private behaviour.
- **Chaharshanbe Suri** (fire-jumping festival, last Wednesday before Nowruz): ancient pre-Islamic ritual, semi-public, liminal — a compressed dramatic space.

### 11. Story-doctoring — diagnostic tools and prescriptions (نسخه‌پیچی داستان)

#### Diagnostic questions (ask these before prescribing)

**Structure diagnosis**:
1. Can you state the controlling idea in one sentence? If not: the theme is unfocused.
2. Where is the inciting incident? If after 15% of runtime: the story starts in the wrong place.
3. Is there a midpoint that reverses the momentum? If not: the story will sag from the 40-60% mark.
4. Does each scene begin and end at different value charges? Find all scenes that begin and end identically: they are non-scenes.
5. Is the all-is-lost moment the *worst* thing that could happen to this protagonist specifically (not just generally bad)? If it's generic: the emotional stakes are not personal enough.
6. Does the climax resolve the *controlling idea*, not just the plot? If the plot resolves but the theme doesn't: the ending will feel hollow.

**Character diagnosis**:
7. What does the protagonist *want*? Can you state it in one concrete sentence? Vague wants = passive protagonists.
8. What does the protagonist *need*? Is it in direct conflict with the want? If they don't conflict: no internal arc.
9. Has the ghost / wound been dramatized on screen — not just explained in backstory?
10. Does the antagonist have a coherent moral argument? "Evil for the sake of evil" is not an antagonist — it is a plot device.
11. Can you distinguish the voice of each character purely from their dialogue (cover the character names)? If not: voice differentiation fails.

**Pace and redundancy diagnosis**:
12. List all scenes in Act II. Do any three consecutive scenes land on the same value charge (all negative, all positive)? That is the sag zone: insert a reversal.
13. Are there scenes that deliver the same information as another scene? Keep the most dramatic one; cut the others.
14. What is the length of the longest scene in the script? If it's more than 5 pages / 5 minutes without an internal value shift: it needs to be cut or subdivided.

#### Prescriptions (surgery options)

**Passive protagonist**: The protagonist reacts but doesn't act. Prescription: give them a clear, specific *want* with a self-imposed *deadline*. Force them to take an action that makes things worse before they make them better. The protagonist must cause at least one of their own problems.

**Sagging middle (Act II sag)**: Apply the Sequence Approach. Identify which of the 8 sequences has no local dramatic question. Give that sequence a tight question and a hard deadline. Alternatively: bring forward a major reversal from Act III to the midpoint.

**The "glass wall" problem**: the protagonist doesn't try hard enough, doesn't push against the obstacles. The story has no escalation because the obstacles are not really obstacles. Prescription: raise the cost of every action. Every choice must have a consequence. Every tactic the protagonist tries must fail before a harder one works.

**Unearned ending**: the climax resolves without the protagonist doing the hardest thing. Prescription: identify what the protagonist is most afraid of doing. Make them do exactly that at the climax. The climax must cost the protagonist something real.

**Thin antagonist**: the villain exists to be defeated. Prescription: write the antagonist as the protagonist of their own story, with a coherent moral argument that *the audience can understand, even if they disagree*. The best antagonists are right about something.

**On-the-nose dialogue**: characters say exactly what they feel. Prescription: for every on-the-nose line, ask "what would this character say instead, if they couldn't say the true thing?" The answer is the subtext line. The on-the-nose line goes in the margin — it tells you what the scene is about, but the scene says something else.

**"Ordinary idea" → "compelling story"**: find the *universal* in the *specific*. A film about one grandmother's secret is a film about all the secrets families carry. A film about one man's failed restaurant is a film about the gap between the life we imagined and the life we got. The specific is the door. The universal is what the audience walks through it into. Your job: find the specific detail so precise that it clicks the universal open.
