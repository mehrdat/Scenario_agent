# Naghd — Critique of *The Last Cheetahs You've Never Heard Of*
## نقد — یوز ایرانی · آخرین‌ها

> Subject: completed scenario at `output/senaario/Persian-Cheetah/06-filmnaameh.md` (post-behtar).
> Method: structural, character, dialogue/voice, and genre diagnosis with scene/timecode references.
> **Verdict header:** this is a strong, disciplined essay documentary. Its discipline is also its primary risk. The critique below is harsher than the praise. Both are deserved.

---

## 1. STRUCTURE

### What works

**The 7-section spine holds.** Each section has a clear local dramatic question and a measurable value-charge shift. The setup-to-payoff grid is tight:
- The piano motif planted at 1:42 (Scene 11, end of Section 2) is paid off at 6:30 (Scene 30, Pirouz's death). Pure Aristotelian discipline.
- The African-sprint opening (Scene 01) is answered exactly by the Iranian-mother closing (Scene 34b). Bookend earned.
- Kooshki's death cause (kidney failure, Scene 24) sets up Pirouz's death cause (kidney failure, Scene 30). The generational rhyme is the film's quietest and most powerful structural move.
- The "we / us" discipline — zero first-person plural narration until Mehrdad's address in Scene 34a — is the film's most disciplined choice. Save-the-Cat's "theme stated" beat is delivered structurally rather than rhetorically.

**The midpoint is correctly positioned.** Pirouz's death lands at 6:30, the 68% mark of a 9:30 runtime — slightly past true midpoint (which would be 4:45) but appropriate for a documentary essay where the All-Is-Lost is the film's centre of gravity. The film correctly fuses *midpoint reversal* and *all-is-lost* into a single beat because the runtime cannot afford two.

**The grammar rule** (every section opens on landscape, closes on a face) is consistently applied across all seven sections. This is rare. Most documentaries lose this discipline in the middle act.

### What is at risk

**Section 4 is the longest section (120 sec) and bears the most narrative load.** It must compress: two thousand years of Sassanid history → Indian extinction 1947 → 1979 revolution → eight-year war → modern roads → roadside carcass. The risk is that the Sassanid opening (Scene 17, newly added in behtar) and the historical chain (Scenes 17a–20) read as a list rather than a chain. **Diagnosis:** the chain currently lacks *causal connective tissue* in the narration. *"For two thousand years, kings rode beside her."* → *"The killing began as a sport."* — the leap from royal partnership to killing is not motivated in narration. A connecting half-sentence (e.g., *"Then the kings became hunters."*) would make the chain a chain. **Prescription:** add one transitional line between Scene 17 and Scene 17a.

**Section 6 has a structural hinge problem.** The narrator's line *"And still — in a riverbed at dawn, in a place no one is watching — she is still here"* (Scene 33c/d) is the bridge from Dark Night to Break-into-Three. The danger is that this hinge feels earned rhetorically but not visually. The audience has just absorbed Seyed-Emami's death, three icon overlays of pressure, and a pull-quote card — and is now asked to feel hope on the strength of one camera-trap shot of an unidentified Yuz and a daylight wadi. **Diagnosis:** the hinge is currently 30 seconds; it may need to be 45. The cinematographer's restraint will determine whether the line lands. **Prescription:** if the rough cut feels rhetorical, extend Scene 33c (the Yuz at dawn) by 6 seconds at the cost of trimming the icon-overlay sequence (Scene 33a) from 14 to 8.

**Pacing of Section 5 is generous.** With Delbar, Koushki II, and Telma now each occupying their own 12-second beat (rather than the draft's combined 24-second sweep), Section 5 has grown to ~120 seconds. The risk is the audience experiences five name cards in succession as a *recitation* rather than five distinct introductions. **Diagnosis:** the script's solution — one sentence per cat — is correct as text, but the cinematographer must vary the *visual rhythm* across the five cards (different photograph framings, different Ken Burns push directions, different durations within the 12-second window). If all five are static portraits with name cards, the audience will glaze. **Prescription:** in the storyboard, vary visual approach across the five — e.g., Kooshki = behind mesh; Delbar = half-turned away; Iran = with cub (if licensed); Koushki II = eye-level direct; Telma = with collar visible. Already noted in `shotlist.md`.

### What does not work

**The film's total runtime estimate is ~9:42 post-behtar, against a target of 9:30.** This is within tolerance but assumes optimal edit discipline. If the rough cut runs 10:00+, the candidates for trim are clear:
1. Trim Sec 4's historical narration by 8 seconds (lose one sentence from the chain).
2. Combine Koushki II + Telma into a single 18-second beat (sacrifice the visual discipline noted above).
3. Trim Mehrdad's address from 30 to 24 seconds (lose one sentence — the *"And to wait for a seventh"* line is the most expendable, though it earns the title card).

Option 1 is the cleanest. Options 2 and 3 cost more than they save.

## 2. CHARACTER / SUBJECT

### What works

**The six-name math is correct.** Five living captive cheetahs + one dead beloved cub = six names the audience must carry. Add a seventh (Telma's actual successor, the unnamed wild mother) and the audience cannot hold them all. The film knows this and respects it.

**Kooshki and Pirouz are correctly designed as a generational pair.** The pattern (orphaned cub → captivity → kidney failure) repeats across two generations. The film does not state the rhyme; the audience absorbs it. This is the film's deepest craft.

**The unnamed wild mother in Section 7 is the correct counter-argument.** Without her, the film is a eulogy. With her, the verdict is pending. Her *backward glance* (at ~13 sec in Scene 34b) is the single most important second of footage in the entire film. If the cinematographer cannot deliver that glance, the film loses its argument-against-despair.

### What is at risk

**Delbar is the most under-served subject.** She is now named (newly added in behtar) but her one sentence — *"The one who took her time. For years, she would not breed. And then, against every expectation, she did"* — does the heavy lifting of an entire 12-second beat. **Diagnosis:** the line is good. The portrait will determine whether she lands. If the archival photograph of Delbar is weak or generic, the audience will receive her as filler between Kooshki (the icon) and Iran (the news). **Prescription:** secure the strongest possible Delbar photograph before lock. If no strong photograph exists, the cinematographer must compose the name card with extra visual weight (longer push, slightly larger card, lower-third dwell).

**Seyed-Emami's beat (Scene 32) is brave but isolated.** The film names a real human being who died in custody. The audience response will split — international viewers may not register the weight; Iranian viewers will register it as the film's bravest moment. **Diagnosis:** the line *"Their crime was a camera in a tree"* (Scene 32a) is doing the work of two sentences. If the mix engineer rushes the pause before *"in a tree"*, the line will pass un-felt. **Prescription:** mark in the master script that this line carries a minimum 1.5-second pause before *"in a tree"*. Protect it through delivery.

### What does not work

**Iran (the female, Scene 26) is the weakest named portrait.** Her one sentence — *"They named her for her country. She would be the first"* — leans entirely on the symbolic weight of her name and the foreshadowing of "the first." The line feels written to set up Pirouz rather than to honor Iran as her own subject. **Diagnosis:** the film treats Iran as a node in the Pirouz arc rather than as a subject in her own right. **Prescription:** add one specific sensory or biographical detail. The character bible notes she was "post-partum thin, watching the humans more than the cubs." A single line — *"She watched the humans more than her cubs"* — would give Iran her own interiority and would, paradoxically, deepen the Pirouz beat that follows. This is the cleanest single edit the script can still absorb.

## 3. DIALOGUE & VOICE

### What works

**The narrator's register is consistent and disciplined throughout.** Five-axis voice differentiation against Mehrdad's outro is achieved:
- **Vocabulary:** the narrator uses informed-elder English; Mehrdad uses conversational Iranian-English.
- **Sentence length:** the narrator's sentences average 8–14 words with deliberate parallel structures; Mehrdad's are 6–10 words with sentence fragments.
- **Idiolect markers:** narrator uses the *"She [verbs]"* construction repeatedly (she lives, she hunts, she is still here); Mehrdad uses *"I made this because..."*.
- **Relationship to truth:** narrator never editorializes; Mehrdad addresses, asks, requests.
- **Taboo zone:** narrator never says *we / us*; Mehrdad uses *we* twice in 30 seconds. The contrast is the film's craft.

**The Mehrdad address is correctly under-written.** Five sentences. The discipline is the meaning. *"I am asking you to remember six names. And to wait for a seventh."* is the film's argument compressed into 14 words. This works.

### What is at risk

**The narrator's parenthetical directions are unusually load-bearing for a documentary script.** *"(half a step quieter)"* before Scene 30a, *"(returns, very softly)"* before Scene 34b — these are not just stage directions; they are the film's tonal architecture. **Diagnosis:** if the recording session does not honor these directions exactly, the film's emotional architecture collapses. The narrator must be cast specifically for the ability to deliver register shifts at this level. **Prescription:** in pre-production, audition narrators on the *register shift* between Scenes 29 and 30a — not just on baseline read. If a candidate cannot deliver half-a-step-quieter on cue, they are not the narrator.

### What does not work

**Scene 17 (the Sassanid opening) has a possible voice slippage.** The line *"For two thousand years, kings rode beside her. They called her Yuz — and they trained her to hunt with them"* — the *"with them"* construction is slightly archaic for the narrator's register. **Diagnosis:** the rest of the narration is contemporary measured English; this line briefly slips toward literary. **Prescription:** consider *"They called her Yuz — and they hunted at her side"* as alternate. Minor — but the slip is the kind of thing that becomes audible in a final mix.

**The Mehrdad line *"And because almost no one outside Iran knows we have a cheetah"* contains the film's only "we" before the second "we" of the closing.** This is *intentional* — both *we*'s are Iranian-national — but the second *we* (*"because if you, watching this, never know her name..."* → wait, the second *we* is implicit, not stated). Re-read: Mehrdad uses *we* once (*"we have a cheetah"*) and addresses the audience as *you* twice. The first-person-plural discipline holds. **Verdict:** my earlier worry is unfounded. The voice is correct.

## 4. GENRE / MODE

### What works

**The Kapadia archival method is correctly executed.** No on-camera interviews (after behtar's fixes to Sections 3 and 6). The narrator-from-above + archive + camera-trap structure is held cleanly through 8 minutes 30 seconds. The Varda-style essayist outro (Mehrdad on camera) is the *single* mode-break the film permits itself, and it is positioned correctly — at the structural Break-into-Three, where the film must address the audience to earn the final image.

**The Nichols mode is honestly hybrid.** This is an Expository documentary (narrator + archive + argument) that becomes Performative (subjective, advocational) in the final 60 seconds. The film does not pretend to be observational. It owns its argument. This is the correct ethical posture.

**The film correctly refuses interview material.** Per the documentary variant brief, no interview questions were generated. The film's argument is carried by narration and archive — not by talking heads. If a future cut requires interview material, the user should explicitly invoke `/mosaahebeh Persian-Cheetah` — but the current cut does not need it.

### What is at risk

**The film's "essay" mode places significant load on the narrator's craft.** A weak narrator destroys an essay documentary. Reference recordings: Tilda Swinton's narration on *We Need to Talk About Kevin*; Cate Blanchett on *Voyage of Time*; Persian-side, the IRIB Mostanad chief narrator's restraint. **Prescription:** budget for narrator casting as if it were a lead role. It is.

**The pull-quote in Scene 33b is a craft compromise.** It replaces an on-camera interview (cut by behtar) but introduces an authored voice into a film that has otherwise denied authored voices. **Diagnosis:** a pull-quote card is acceptable but it does mark a brief departure from the archival-only discipline. **Prescription:** if the ICS biologist's full name and permission can be secured, attribute the quote with the name. The named attribution converts the pull-quote from "unsourced wisdom" into "documented testimony" and restores the archival contract.

### What does not work

**The film addresses a Western audience but uses Iranian cultural specifics it cannot fully explain.** The word *Yuz* is given pause and emphasis (Scene 04, the new "full beat before Yuz"); the Pirouz death is treated as already-significant; Seyed-Emami's death is named without contextualizing the espionage charge. **Diagnosis:** the film expects the international audience to read Iranian grief without prior knowledge. This is partly a strength (the audience is treated as intelligent) and partly a risk (key context is unexplained). **Prescription:** add a 4-second lower-third under Scene 32 stating *"Founder of the Persian Wildlife Heritage Foundation. Imprisoned January 2018."* This sentence is currently delivered in narration but flashes by; the lower-third anchors it visually for the international audience.

## 6. ETHICAL AUDIT

The documentary handles ethically sensitive material:

- **Real animals** (Kooshki, Delbar, Iran, Koushki II, Telma, Pirouz): the script does not fabricate biography. All factual claims are sourced in the research note. **Pass.**
- **A real human death in custody** (Seyed-Emami): the script states documented facts only. No reconstruction. No editorial speculation about the cause of death. **Pass.**
- **The roadside carcass image** (Scene 22): handled with restraint. Wide-only, no close-up, 4 seconds, no exploitation. **Pass.**
- **The empty incubator** (Scene 30): the death is signified by absence, not by Pirouz's body. **Pass.**
- **The wild family** (Scene 34b): the cubs are deliberately unnamed because anonymity is their safety in conservation terms. **Pass.**
- **AI-generated content**: the prompt-video file imposes strict rules — no AI-generated portraits of named animals, no AI-generated portraits of Seyed-Emami, no AI-generated "archival" footage. **Pass, with the caveat that the production team must enforce these rules.**

**One open ethical question:** the film states *"Other colleagues remain in prison"* (Scene 32a) without naming them. The choice to not name them may be a protective gesture (avoiding further attention on imprisoned figures); it may also be insufficient acknowledgment. **Recommendation:** consult with ICS / PWHF before lock about whether to name specific imprisoned colleagues (with their or their families' permission) or to retain the protective collective phrasing.

## 7. THE FILM IF IT FAILS

If this film fails, it will fail in one of three ways:

**Failure 1: the narrator does not deliver the register shifts.** The film lives or dies on the narrator's voice. A boom-voice "documentary narrator" will destroy it. A weak whisper will destroy it. The narrator must hold informed-elder authority for 8 minutes 30 seconds and then disappear when Mehrdad enters frame. This is a hard cast.

**Failure 2: the archival access does not materialize.** The film depends on real photographs of six named cheetahs, real archival footage of Pirouz, a real news photograph of Seyed-Emami, and real camera-trap footage of a wild female with five cubs. If any of the *named* archival assets are unobtainable, the corresponding beat must collapse to a name card. If the *wild mother + five cubs* footage is unobtainable, the film should cut the beat and end on Mehrdad's address — never fabricate the closing image.

**Failure 3: the international audience does not know enough Iranian context to feel the weight.** The film does not over-explain. A viewer who has never heard of Iran's wildlife crisis may experience the film as elegant but distant. **The pull-quote card in Scene 33b is the one place to consider adding international context** — possibly converting it from *"Every camera we put in a tree is an act of patience"* to a longer-form on-screen card with one sentence of background. **Recommendation: leave the pull-quote as is.** The audience that needs context will seek it; the audience that already knows will be honored. The discipline serves both.

## 8. THE FILM IF IT SUCCEEDS

If this film succeeds, it will be because:

1. The audience can recite *Kooshki, Delbar, Iran, Koushki, Telma, Pirouz* when the lights come up.
2. The blank seventh line on the final title card will feel like an obligation, not a gimmick.
3. The misdirection of the African opening will retroactively read as the audience's own complicity — they had the wrong picture in their head, and the film corrected it.
4. Mehrdad's address will not feel preachy. It will feel like a friend asking a small thing.
5. A viewer who has never thought about Iranian wildlife will look up *Asiatic cheetah* before bed.

These outcomes are achievable. The script supports all five. The production must execute.
