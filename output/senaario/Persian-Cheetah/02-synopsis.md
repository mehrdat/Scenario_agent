# Synopsis — The Last Cheetah You've Never Heard Of
## یوز — آخرین‌ها

**Runtime:** 9:30 · **Aspect:** 16:9 · **Voice:** BBC omniscient → Mehrdad personal (outro only)

---

## ACT ONE — The Misdirection and the Reveal (0:00 – 1:50)

The film opens on the image every viewer expects: a cheetah at full sprint across golden African savanna, dust kicked from her paws, a gazelle bolting in the frame ahead. The narrator's voice — calm, measured, in the cadence the world has learned from David Attenborough — begins to describe the fastest land animal on Earth. For eight seconds, this is a film we have seen before.

Then the image cuts. The savanna becomes a stone plateau under a colder sun. The grass becomes scrub. The horizon is no longer Kenyan acacia but the dry mountain ridges of central Iran. The narrator pauses — and asks the audience to imagine a cheetah that does not live where they think.

This is the **Asiatic cheetah** — *Acinonyx jubatus venaticus*, the **Yuz (یوز)** — and there are fewer than thirty of them left in the world. All of them are here. All of them are Iranian.

A short, controlled passage of comparative biology follows: the Yuz is leaner than her African cousin, longer-legged, with a thicker winter coat and a smaller skull. She is built not for grass but for **stone**. The narrator names the prey: jebeer gazelle, wild sheep, hare. Names the elevation: 1,000 to 3,000 metres. Names the climate: snow in winter, forty-five degrees Celsius in summer. The film has now told the audience what it is about. The misdirection is closed. The wound opens.

## ACT TWO — The World, the Wound, the Names (1:50 – 8:30)

**The world (1:50 – 3:00).** The narrator establishes the geography that has both protected and isolated this cat: the protected areas of **Touran**, **Miandasht**, **Naybandan**, and **Kavir**. Drone-style wides over pale gravel and snow-dusted ridges. Camera-trap footage shows a single male moving along a dry riverbed at three in the morning. The cheetah is presented not as a species but as a **place** — a creature inseparable from a specific Iranian topography that most of the world has never seen on screen. The piano motif is planted here, a single sustained string, to be paid off seventy seconds before the end.

**The wound (3:00 – 5:00).** The film turns historical. A century ago the Asiatic cheetah ranged from the Arabian Peninsula to India; the last Indian cheetahs were shot in 1947. In Iran, the Qajar and Pahlavi hunting culture, then the chaos of the 1979 revolution, then the eight-year war with Iraq, then the explosive growth of road infrastructure across central Iran — each blow named and dated. The single deadliest cause of Yuz death in the twenty-first century is shown plainly: vehicle collision. The Tehran–Mashhad highway cuts directly through the species' last viable corridor. A held still of a Yuz carcass at a roadside is shown just long enough to register, not long enough to exploit — four seconds, then the score drops out, and only wind remains.

**The names (5:00 – 7:00).** This is the emotional spine of the film. Six names, each given the dignity of a portrait card and one sentence:

**Kooshki** (کوشکی), the male captured young in 2007 when his mother was killed by herders — the orphan who became the face of the species and died of kidney failure in 2018. **Delbar** (دلبر), the female who would not breed for years and then, against every expectation, did. **Iran** (ایران), the female named for her country, the first Asiatic cheetah to give birth in captivity. **Koushki II** (کوشکی دوم), the male of the next generation. **Telma** (تلما), the matriarch whose tracking-collar drew the first true map of how a Yuz uses her land.

And then **Pirouz** (پیروز — *Victorious*) — born on **1 May 2022**, the first Asiatic cheetah cub born in captivity, broadcast to a nation that had not had good news about its wildlife in a generation. Two of his siblings died within fourteen days; the film names this loss in one sentence and holds for a beat of silence on a gloved hand with a syringe. For three hundred days, Pirouz was a small, public miracle. Children drew him. State television covered his weight gain. His mother's milk supply, his first solid food — all of it followed by a country watching its own hope grow whiskers.

**The All-Is-Lost beat lands at approximately 6:30.** Pirouz dies of kidney failure at ten months. The film does not soften this. A still photograph of an empty veterinary incubator. A held nine-second silence. The piano motif from Section 3 returns — now joined by a single sustained cello note that will become Pirouz's signature. The narrator, half a step quieter: *He was named Victorious. He lived ten months.*

**The dark night (7:00 – 8:30).** Conservation today is harder than it has ever been. International sanctions starve the programme of veterinary equipment and exchange. Senior Iranian wildlife biologist **Kavous Seyed-Emami** dies in custody in 2018 — arrested for the crime of putting cameras in trees. Other colleagues remain in prison. The 2026 regional war pulls budget, attention, and personnel away from the protected areas. The roads keep killing. The narrator does not editorialise; she states. The audience is allowed to feel.

But then — in a riverbed at dawn, in a place no one is watching — she is still here. Camera-trap footage from autumn 2023: a wild female on a Touran ridge with cubs behind her. The grain is real. The cats are real. The film has not lied to the audience, and so the audience may believe what comes next.

## ACT THREE — The Final Image (8:30 – 9:30)

The narrator falls silent. The film cuts to **Mehrdad**, the filmmaker, sitting in his own room, in his own daylight, looking directly into the lens. He speaks in his own voice — quieter, unscripted in cadence, personal. He says: *I made this because I am Iranian. And because almost no one outside Iran knows we have a cheetah. And because if you, watching this, never know her name, she will go quietly. I am asking you to remember six names. And to wait for a seventh.*

He cuts back to the camera-trap footage: a single wild female with five cubs, moving in line along a Touran ridge at dusk. The mother turns once to check on them. The film closes on a final title card on black:

> **Kooshki · Delbar · Iran · Koushki · Telma · Pirouz (2022 — 2023)**
>
> ***___________***
>
> Fewer than 30 remain. Five were born this year.

Wind on the ridge. Hold black three seconds. Cut to black. End.

---

## Why this story, this way

The film exists because the Asiatic cheetah is going extinct quietly — and *quietly* is the enemy. A documentary about a species nobody knows by name cannot ask the audience to care about a species. It can only ask them to care about a Kooshki, a Pirouz, a wild mother with five cubs. The film is structured to make that transfer happen — from genus to individual, from statistic to name, from silence to address. The opening misdirection (Africa) and the closing reveal (one Iranian mother) bookend the controlling idea: *the wrong picture has been in your head for thirty years. Here is the right one. Now you know.*
